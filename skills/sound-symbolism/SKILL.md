# Sound Symbolism

Brand names are sounds before they are words. Each letter of the English alphabet carries a *perceptual quality* — a small bundle of associations and physical sensations — that humans pick up before they decode meaning. A name composed of the right sounds can carry its intended experience even when its dictionary content is opaque. A name composed of the wrong sounds will fight its own meaning.

This skill gives you the vocabulary and the table to reason about names by sound.

## The English letter table

These mappings are based on cross-cultural research into how listeners perceive isolated letters in name-like contexts. They are *defaults* — a name with a leading V starts with vibrancy as its baseline. The surrounding context can shift the reading, but the baseline is real.

|--------|---------|---------------------|
| **A** | Open, accessible, expansive | Apple, Amazon, Atlas |
| **B** | Reliable, solid, grounded, dependable | Blackberry (double-B), Bose, Brita |

| **D** | Determined, declarative, sometimes blunt | Dell, Disney, Dyson |

Notes:

- Quality is *strongest at the start of a name*. A leading V hits the listener with vibrancy. A V in the middle (`Toyota Avalon`) is much weaker.
- Doubled letters intensify (`Blackberry`'s double-B doubles reliability; `Kodak`'s K-frame doubles sharpness).
- The vowel between consonants colors the reading. `Sano` reads calmer than `Sano!` than `Sani` than `Sano-X`.
- Hard vs soft C and G follow normal English rules, and the table entry that applies is the one the average reader will produce on first sight.

## The compound multiplier

`Powerbook`, `Blackberry`, `Facebook`, `Windsurf`. When you join two real words, you do not simply add their meanings. The associations multiply. `Power` carries a cluster of associations. `Book` carries another. The compound creates the cross-product — *a powerful book, a book about power, the kind of person who reads books of power.*

Lexicon's measured rule of thumb: **compounds are roughly 3× as evocative as their parts in isolation.** This is why when a client says "I'd prefer a single word, the compound is too long," the right response is to surface the multiplier effect.

The compound works hardest when:

- Both halves are common-vocabulary words (`Blackberry`, not `Phenylpyruvate-berry`).
- The two halves come from different semantic spheres (`Wind` + `surf` is sport; `Sky` + `hook` crosses domains).
- The compound is plausibly spelled as one word (`Windsurf`, not `Wind-Surf`).
- The compound is two to four syllables total. Past four, the multiplier degrades.

## Rhythm

Stress and syllable count carry their own signal independent of consonants.

- **Strong-weak (trochaic), two syllables:** `Pen-tium`, `Ja-guar`, `Ne-tflix`. Punchy, confident, frontloaded. Reads decisive.
- **Weak-strong (iambic), two syllables:** `Cor-vette`, `Re-spond`. Building, momentum, arrival. Reads with motion.
- **Three syllables, strong-weak-weak:** `Black-ber-ry`, `Hon-da-da` (not real but you hear it). Friendly, balanced, easy.
- **Three syllables, weak-strong-weak:** `Da-sa-ni`, `Ma-zda-ni`. Calming, centered, premium.
- **Single syllable, single hit:** `Stripe`, `Slack`, `Lyft`. Maximum punch. Hard to forget.
- **Four+ syllables:** `Mer-ce-des-Benz`, `An-thro-pic`. Authority and weight, but processing-fluency cost.

Match the rhythm to the framework. A "calming flow" experience wants weak-strong-weak. A "fast and innovative" experience wants single-syllable or strong-weak.

## Palindromes and near-palindromes

`Sonos`, `Aviva`, `Aha`, `Eve`, `Pop`. Palindromes are memorable because the brain processes them as confirmed — the second half ratifies the first.

Sonos is the case study Placek tells: not only spells the same backwards, but typeset flips upside down and still reads. The palindrome carries an extra signal — *symmetry, completion, deliberate craft*. It works especially well for brands whose product carries a sense of pairing or duality (Sonos: speakers in stereo; Aviva: insurance pairs).

Near-palindromes (`Halal`, `Roto`, `Madam`) carry partial credit.

## Processing fluency

The brain is lazy. Names that are easy to:

- Pronounce on first sight
- Spell after hearing once
- Type from memory
- Recognize at a glance

…are *preferred* over names that are harder, all else equal. The cognitive cost of a hard name compounds with every mention. A name a customer mispronounces feels foreign forever.

Reasons names hit fluency walls:

- **Gnarly consonant clusters:** `Schmptz`, `Thwxn`. Avoid.
- **Ambiguous vowels:** Is `Levo` pronounced `LEE-voh` or `LEH-voh`? If both are plausible, drop.
- **Foreign borrowings the target audience cannot pronounce:** `Aujourd'hui` for an English-language market.
- **Made-up spellings of real words:** `Kwik`, `Lyte`. These work in 1970s consumer goods. They feel dated now.

But: a small fluency cost is acceptable when the payoff is high. `Häagen-Dazs` is hard to read and impossible to spell, and it works because the difficulty signals *imported, premium, deliberately marked*. The rule is not "always make it easy." The rule is "if you spend fluency, make sure you bought something."

## How to use this skill

### In Identify

When writing the creative framework's "rhythm and sound priorities" section, translate experience verbs into letter and rhythm priorities.

- "Alive and bold" → V, X, Z. Strong-weak rhythm. Two or three syllables.
- "Calm and trustworthy" → L, M, N, B. Weak-strong-weak. Three or four syllables.
- "Fast and modern" → X, K, Z, hard C. Single syllable or strong-weak.
- "Premium and grounded" → B, R, H, M. Weak-strong-weak. Foreign-borrowing acceptable.

### In Invent

Use the table to *bias* your generation. If the framework calls for V-led names, generate 100+ V-led candidates explicitly. Then generate without constraint for variety.

### In Screen

For each candidate, write a one-line sound-symbolism reading: "V-alive + rolling-R + open-A → vibrant, masculine, expansive." This becomes the rationale-doc fodder later.

### In Recommend

When defending a name to a stakeholder, lead with sound symbolism. *"Azure starts with the open A, lands on Z which is noisy and modern, ends on the round closing -ure. It signals open expansion, attention-getting innovation, and arrival. Cloud-Pro starts and ends descriptively. It signals what you are, not who you are."* Stakeholders respect the analysis even when they cannot articulate it themselves.

## Anti-uses

- Do not treat the table as deterministic. A name with five V's is not five times more vibrant; it is annoying. Read the table as *baseline*; the surrounding context modulates.
- Do not apply English sound symbolism to names targeting non-English-primary markets without checking. The cultural-vetting screen (see `name-screening` skill) catches the worst failures.
- Do not use this skill to *generate* names in isolation. It is a screen, a vocabulary, and a defender. Generation also needs morpheme play, foreign borrowings, mythology, compounds — sound symbolism is one layer, not the whole stack.
